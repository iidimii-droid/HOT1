## THESIS (the spine of the video)
Using tools is *not* what makes humans unique — many animals do it. What separates us is the ability to **imagine a tool that doesn't yet exist, and then make it.** That single capacity — planned, foresightful making — is the origin of all technology.

Franklin's line (1778) frames it: humans are the "tool-making animal."

## FACT BANK (by theme)

### A. Animals that USE tools
- **Burrowing wasp (Ammophila):** the female tamps soil/sand over her nest, and has been seen gripping a small pebble in her jaws to hammer the surface flat and erase all trace of the nest.
- **Darwin's finch (Galápagos):** holds a cactus spine or twig lengthwise in its beak to poke insects out of bark, then drops the spine to grab the prey; picks prickly-pear spines in dry zones, snaps twigs where cacti don't grow, and rejects twigs that are too short or too bendy.
- **Sea otter (off California):** floats on its back, rests a flat stone (~15–20 cm) on its chest as an anvil, and smashes hard-shelled molluscs (e.g. abalone) against it; studied by Edna Fisher.
- **Greater spotted woodpecker (Britain):** jams pine cones into a V-shaped cleft in a trunk — a natural "vice" — to hold them while extracting seeds; also uses the clefts on galls to dig out insects for its young.
- **Satin bower-bird (Australia):** the male paints his bower with pulp from coloured fruit.
- **Baboons:** ground-dwellers; use pebbles to kill scorpions, roll stones at pursuers, and (reported in Zululand) join organised hunts to corner and kill small antelope.

### B. Instinct vs. learning; "tradition" in animals
- Most insect behaviour is instinct, released by set stimuli, with only slight flexibility (a wasp may use a pebble only occasionally).
- Rigidity example: a caterpillar whose half-built cocoon is removed just keeps building the second half.
- Birds are largely instinctive but adaptable; canaries bred in isolation for five generations still built proper nests in the sixth. Play and trial-and-error refine skill (a kitten's play → an adult cat's hunting).
- **Culture in animals:** after some tits learned to open milk-bottle tops, the habit spread across Britain — behaviour passed by imitation, i.e. a kind of "tradition."

### C. Apes and the imagination limit (the near-miss)
- **Chimp "Sultan" (Köhler, Tenerife, 1913–17):** fitted one bamboo cane into another to make a pole long enough to reach a banana; once shaped a stick with his teeth to make it fit. Apes *can* improvise and briefly make tools — **but only with the reward in sight.**
- Take the reward away and the idea evaporates. Köhler's point: the chimp lives in a very narrow slice of time (little sense of past/future); lacking speech and mental "images," it can't build culture.
- Mme Kohts (Moscow): a chimp could sort by colour across varied shapes — a flicker of abstract thought — but the idea faded fast.
- A capuchin monkey once scored as high as a chimp on an intelligence test (some monkeys are as quick-witted as apes).

### D. The human leap: conceptual thought
- Humans can picture a tool that isn't there and hold that image until it's built; we can pull ideas out of memory and rearrange them — the prerequisite of planning and invention.
- Framing device: the toolmaker is an **artist in Aristotle's sense** — conceiving the finished form before making it.
- The defining act: **using a tool to make another tool** (a hammerstone to shape a hand-axe; a struck flake to carve a wooden spear).

### E. Why us — the body that made it possible
- Skill depends on: sensory perception, coordinating past + present impressions, physical capacity, and environmental demand.
- **Trees came first:** early primates (from tiny Cretaceous insectivores) evolved five grasping digits and an increasingly mobile, opposable first digit; forelimbs took over jobs from jaws and teeth — "hands," in effect.
- **Vision over smell:** arboreal life favoured sight; eyes moved forward → **stereoscopic (depth) vision.** The tarsier is a "living fossil" of this stage. The *macula lutea* (a sharp-vision retinal spot in primates, carnivores, birds) enables the close scrutiny that manipulation needs.
- **Brain:** the cortex/association areas store and combine memory (→ reasoning, planning); a large motor area controls purposive movement, much of it devoted to the hands; frontal/temporal lobes enlarged in humans.

### F. Standing up — and the surprise about skill
- Walking upright freed the hands (to carry, gather, make); the pelvis reshaped for the upright gait, the foot became a rigid support, legs outgrew arms.
- The human hand is **generalised and "primitive"** — less specialised than an ape's (apes' hands are built for swinging). We walk on our palms; apes on their knuckles.
- **Key insight:** skill is mainly *cerebral*, not manual. People with coarse hands can be superb craftsmen, and people who've lost hands develop remarkable dexterity — "manual" skill really lives in the brain and the eye–hand wiring. (Wood Jones: our place in nature is "writ upon the hand.")

### G. Origins of deliberate tool-making
- Meat-eating is about as old as humanity; early Palaeolithic people were hunters. On open grassland (drought, few trees) proto-humans — like baboons — turned to flesh, cooperation, and cunning, hunting in groups.
- With no claws or fangs, they needed **sharp stone** to cut hide and flesh → breaking pebbles on purpose to make edges → the tool-making tradition. As tools took over these jobs, canine teeth shrank.
- Becoming semi-carnivorous freed time (a meat diet needs less bulk than constant grazing) → more hunting, more social interdependence, more skills.
- **The big payoff:** tools/weapons/shelter/clothing are "extra-bodily equipment" you can change in a lifetime — while bodily evolution takes hundreds of thousands of years. That made humans the most adaptable creature, able to live in every climate.

### H. Language as an invented tool
- Speech is itself "a tool that had to be invented." Naming things lets the mind isolate and recombine ideas (Helen Keller's realisation at ~7 that "everything has a name").
- Early communication may have been by gesture (hand–mouth movements in chimps and children), shifting from manual to spoken gesture; the earliest words were probably actions, then names.
- Speech isn't tied to a special brain "centre"; it's disturbed by injury along the dominant (usually left) hemisphere — which also controls the right hand, linking right-handedness with language. Toolmaking and speech are, in a sense, two forms of the same thing.

### I. The six stages of tool culture (a clean ladder for the video)
1. Occasional use of found/improvised tools ("Eolithic") — *Australopithecus* / Pliocene hominids.
2. Occasional tool-making — dawn of the Lower Palaeolithic.
3. Regular tool-making, little standardisation — e.g. Pekin man.
4. Regular tool-making, standardised — early *Homo sapiens* forerunners (Kanam, Swanscombe).
5. Specialised tools/weapons — (a) elementary: Middle Palaeolithic, Neanderthals; (b) composite: Upper Palaeolithic/Mesolithic, Cro-Magnons.
6. Machine principles (Neolithic/Metal Ages) — modern humans.

### J. The stone-tool story (sites · species · industries)
- **Eoliths:** naturally cracked stones can't be told from human work — so the very first tools are essentially unidentifiable.
- **Pekin man** (*Homo erectus*), **Zhoukoudian/Choukoutien**, China: thousands of imported broken stones; broke lumps on a stone/bone anvil with a hammerstone, mostly using the flakes; rough choppers and worked bone; better raw material (chert) in upper layers ≠ better skill; **used fire regularly**; hunted deer, horse, bison, rhino, elephant, bear. Most tools chipped by right-handers (brain lateralisation). ("Dexterity" ← Latin *dexter*, "on the right.")
- **Kanam, Kenya:** pebble-tools with a near-modern human jaw → early *H. sapiens* forerunners made them.
- **Olduvai, Tanzania:** pebble-tool culture → **Chelleo-Acheulian** hand-axes.
- **Hand-axes (Acheulian):** flaked on both faces into a pointed, tongue-shaped, all-purpose tool — the **first standardised implement**; unhafted; used for ~100,000 years across roughly a fifth of the globe. Acheulian hand-axes from the Cape, Kenya, Madras and London are nearly identical regardless of stone — uniformity born of tradition/imitation.
- **Swanscombe, Kent (1935–6):** the only sure skull of a hand-axe maker — essentially modern, >100,000 years old.
- **Core-tools vs flake-tools:** Clactonian (Europe) and Hope Fountain (S. Africa) = primitive flake industries.
- **Oldest surviving wooden weapon:** a fire-pointed **yew-wood spear** shaped with flint (Clacton-on-Sea, Essex); another found inside an elephant skeleton at Lehringen, Germany.
- **Levallois / "tortoise-core":** shape the core first so a struck flake comes off ready-to-use — an efficient, near-final skinning-flake; used widely, including by Neanderthals.
- **Mousterian (Neanderthals):** Ice-Age Europe/West Asia; cave dwellers in pelts; pressure-flaked flake-tools (triangular points, D-shaped side-scrapers, small hand-axes); wooden spears, occasional hafting (a spear-point wound at Mount Carmel); worked bone; fire and cooked meat; right-handed; hunted mammoth, rhino, bear.
- **Upper Palaeolithic (Cro-Magnons):** a sudden explosion of specialised, often **composite** tools and new techniques — parallel-sided blades (punch + hammer), burins for working bone/antler/ivory, hafted spear-heads, the **spear-thrower** (lever) and the **bow** (late, N. Africa) — which also gave the rotary drill. Eyed bone needles → **sewn, fitted skin clothing** (sleeves, trousers). Tents, sunken communal huts for mammoth hunters, and **cave art** painted deep in caverns by stone-lamp light — evidence of acute observation and visual memory. Art appears near the very dawn of toolmaking (Pekin man carried quartz crystals from afar).
- **After:** the Mesolithic, then farming/domestication in the Middle East (food production) → settled communities, new crafts, civilisation.

### K. Fire · clothing · art (the "making our own world" beats)
- **Fire:** the great step toward freedom from environment — warmth, cooked meat, spreading into cold climates.
- **Clothing/shelter:** sewn garments and built shelters let humans colonise every climate.
- **Art:** the artistic impulse shows up almost at the start of toolmaking and blossoms in Upper Palaeolithic cave painting.

### L. The closing insight (handle §L carefully)
- **Usable, strong point:** culture doesn't track brain size — it depends on **shared tradition.** Examples: Neanderthals had *larger* average brains than modern humans but a simpler culture; Swanscombe man had a modern-sized brain yet a static culture for 100,000+ years. Brain-case size/shape doesn't reveal cortical efficiency (Weidenreich compared reading it to "phrenology"). The same technique (Levallois) shows up across species — likely spread by contact, not reinvented each time.
- **Caution for the script:** the chapter also contains **dated 1950s comparisons between "primitive" and "civilised" peoples and races.** Do **not** repeat those. The sound, modern takeaway is the opposite of ranking groups: human capacities are broadly shared, and cultural difference comes from transmitted tradition and environment, not biology. Frame it that way.
