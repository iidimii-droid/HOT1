Baboons kill scorpions with pebbles, roll stones down at anything chasing them, and in Zululand have been seen hunting antelope in organised groups — cutting off escape routes, working together.

Some of this is instinct — behaviour on rails. Take a caterpillar halfway through spinning its cocoon and remove the finished half, and it will not go back. It carries on building the second half, into empty air.

> **09 · PD · 4s** — Blue tits tearing foil milk-bottle caps. Mid-century British newsreel; generate if not findable.

But not all of it is rigid. There is even something like tradition. When a few tits in Britain worked out how to tear the foil caps off doorstep milk bottles, the trick spread — bird to bird, across the country. Not instinct. Not invention, exactly. Imitation. Culture, in its smallest possible form.

# §3 · THE NEAR-MISS — 1:30

Which brings us to the animals closest to us.

> **10 · AI · 8s**
> ```
> Chimpanzee seated on a bare floor holding two bamboo canes, fitting the narrow
> end of one into the hollow end of the other, close on the hands, single window
> light, 1910s research-station interior
> ```

On Tenerife, between 1913 and 1917, Wolfgang Köhler set a chimpanzee named Sultan a problem: a banana out of reach, and two bamboo canes, each too short. Sultan fumbled, gave up, sat down — and then pushed one cane into the hollow end of the other and raked in the fruit. On another occasion he shaved a stick down with his teeth until it fitted.

That is not tool use. That is tool *making*. So why isn't the chimpanzee writing this script?

> **11 · AI · 7s** — *hero shot of the segment*
> ```
> Chimpanzee holding a bamboo cane, looking at an empty doorway where a reward
> used to be, the cane slowly lowering out of frame, attention dissolving, soft
> falloff to black
> ```

Because of one constraint, and it is everything. Sultan could only do it with the banana in sight. Take the reward out of view and the whole idea dissolves. The plan cannot survive the absence of the thing it is for.

Köhler's conclusion was that the chimpanzee lives inside a very narrow window of time — barely any past, almost no future. In Moscow, Mme Kohts found a chimp that could sort objects by colour across completely different shapes, a real flicker of abstraction. But it faded as fast as it appeared.

The ability is there. It just cannot be held onto.

# §4 · THE LEAP — 2:45

Here is the line.

> **12 · MG · 6s** — The imagination gap. `motion_graphics.html?shot=12`

A human being can picture a tool that does not exist, hold that picture steady while the hands work, and keep holding it for hours, or days, or a lifetime. We can reach into memory, pull out pieces, and rearrange them into something that has never been.

> **13 · AI · 8s**
> ```
> Extreme close-up of weathered human hands turning a rough flint nodule over,
> thumb tracing where the edge will be, nothing struck yet, firelight from below
> ```

Aristotle described the artist as someone who conceives the finished form before making it. By that definition, the first person to strike an edge onto a stone was an artist. They saw the tool inside the rock before the rock was touched.

And then comes the stranger step — the one with no equivalent anywhere else in the living world.

Using a tool to make another tool.

> **14 · AI · 8s** — *hero*
> ```
> Close on a hammerstone striking a flint core at a precise angle, a flake spinning
> away in slow motion, chips suspended in low sunlight, hands steady and deliberate
> ```

A hammerstone that exists only to shape a hand-axe. A struck flake that exists only to carve a spear. At that moment technology stops being a single clever act and becomes a process that can build on itself. Everything after this — every engine, every circuit, every machine that makes machines — is that same move, repeated.

# §5 · THE BODY THAT WAS ALREADY READY — 3:45

None of it would have been possible without a body that had already, accidentally, been prepared.

> **16 · AI · 7s**
> ```
> Small nocturnal primate gripping a slender branch high in dense forest canopy,
> five digits wrapping fully around the wood, opposable first digit clearly
> visible, dawn mist below
> ```

Our ancestors were small insect-eating mammals, and they went up into the trees. Everything followed from that.

A canopy rewards gripping. Five digits, and a first digit that grew steadily more mobile and more opposable — until the forelimb could take over work the jaws and teeth had been doing. Not paws. Hands.

> **17 · AI · 6s**
> ```
> Tarsier clinging to a vertical sapling at night, enormous forward-facing eyes
> catching moonlight, head rotating to fix on camera, deep black forest behind
> ```

A canopy also rewards sight over smell, because a branch you cannot see is a branch you fall from. The eyes migrated forward until their fields overlapped, and depth appeared — the world in three dimensions. At the centre of the primate retina sits a small patch of extraordinarily sharp vision. You cannot make a fine tool you cannot properly see.

> **18 · MG · 8s** — Eye → cortex → hand. `motion_graphics.html?shot=18`

Behind both, the cortex was reorganising — expanding where memory is stored and combined, and where deliberate movement is governed, an outsized share of it given to the hands.

> **19 · AI · 7s**
> ```
> Silhouetted early hominin rising from a crouch on open grassland at golden hour,
> hands coming free and empty at its sides, long shadow, heat haze
> ```

Then we stood up, and the hands came free. The pelvis reshaped. The foot stiffened into a platform. For the first time a primate could carry, gather, and *make* while moving.

> **20 · MG · 6s** — Hand comparison. `motion_graphics.html?shot=20`

But here is the surprise, and it overturns what most people assume. Our hands are not specialised. They are generalised — in evolutionary terms almost primitive. An ape's hand is the specialised one, built and tensioned for swinging. We didn't get a better hand. We got a less committed one.

And skill itself turns out not to live in the hand at all. It is cerebral. Craftsmen with thick, coarse, unpromising hands produce exquisite work. People who lose their hands develop dexterity that seems impossible. What we call manual skill is the wiring between eye, brain and hand — and the hand is the least of the three.

# §6 · THE FIRST REAL TOOLS — 5:45

> **21 · AI · 8s**
> ```
> Slow aerial push over a landscape where closed forest breaks apart into open
> savanna, dry season, dust and thinning tree cover, long raking light
> ```

Now put that body somewhere difficult.

The forests thinned. Grassland opened. And on open ground, like the baboons, our ancestors turned to meat — which meant cooperation, and cunning, and hunting in groups.

There was one problem. We have no claws. We have no fangs worth the name. A carcass on the savanna is armoured in hide that bare hands cannot open.

> **22 · AI · 8s** — *hero shot of the episode*
> ```
> Extreme close-up, a hominin hand bringing a hammerstone down hard on a river
> pebble, the pebble splitting to reveal a bright sharp edge, fragments and dust
> in slow motion, firelit
> ```

So: break a stone. Not a stone that happened to be sharp — a stone broken *on purpose*, because you knew what the break would give you. That is the beginning of the tradition, and it never stopped.

The consequences came quickly. As tools took over the work of tearing, the canine teeth shrank. A meat diet needs far less bulk than constant grazing, so time opened up — for hunting, for cooperation, for skills that take a long while to learn.

> **23 · MG · 6s** — Extra-bodily equipment. `motion_graphics.html?shot=23`

And the deepest consequence of all: a tool is equipment outside the body. You can change it inside a single lifetime. Bodily evolution takes hundreds of thousands of years; a new blade takes an afternoon. That asymmetry is the whole story. It made us the most adaptable animal that has ever lived.

# §7 · LANGUAGE, ANOTHER INVENTED TOOL — 7:00

> **24 · AI · 6s**
> ```
> Close on a firelit face mid-gesture, one hand shaping the air while the mouth
> forms a word, other faces watching from the dark beyond the fire
> ```

There is a second tool here, easy to miss, because we are inside it.

Speech had to be invented too. Naming a thing lets the mind lift it out of the world and turn it over. Helen Keller described the moment at around seven when she understood that everything has a name, and the world reorganised itself around that fact.

The earliest communication may have been gesture; you can still see the hand-and-mouth linkage in chimpanzees and in small children. Manual gesture, gradually becoming spoken gesture.

And speech has no single dedicated centre in the brain. It is disrupted by damage along the dominant hemisphere — usually the left, which also governs the right hand. Toolmaking and language run down the same side. Our word *dexterity* comes from the Latin for "on the right." They may be two expressions of one capacity.

# §8 · THE LONG ARC OF STONE — 7:50

Then the record opens up.

> **25 · AI · 7s**
> ```
> Four Acheulian hand-axes laid on dark cloth in a museum vitrine, near-identical
> in form but cut from visibly different stone, raking top light, shallow focus
> racking along the row
> ```

The Acheulian hand-axe — flaked on both faces to a pointed, all-purpose edge. The first standardised tool in history. It was made for roughly a hundred thousand years across about a fifth of the planet, and examples from the Cape, Kenya, Madras and London are nearly interchangeable. Not because the stone was the same. Because the tradition was.

> **26 · AI · 8s**
> ```
> Hands working by firelight, long parallel flint blades laid out in a row, a burin
> carving a groove into reindeer antler, an eyed bone needle beside them
> ```

And then, suddenly, the Upper Palaeolithic. Long parallel blades struck with a punch. Burins for carving bone and antler and ivory. The spear-thrower — a lever. The bow.

> **27 · AI · 7s**
> ```
> Figure in fitted sewn skin clothing with real sleeves walking across wind-driven
> snow, breath visible, Ice-Age steppe stretching behind, low grey light
> ```

Eyed needles, and with them sewn clothing that actually fits, with sleeves, which is what lets a tropical animal walk into an Ice Age and stay. Tents, and sunken huts built by mammoth hunters.

> **28 · AI · 9s** — *hero*

> wall, the light moving so the animals appear to shift, absolute darkness beyond

And deep inside caves, by the light of stone lamps, paintings of animals observed so precisely they could only have been held in memory and put down later, in the dark.

# §9 · PAYOFF — 8:55

One last thing, and it matters.

> **29 · MG · 6s** — Brain size vs culture. `motion_graphics.html?shot=29`

None of this tracks brain size. Neanderthals had larger average brains than we do and a simpler culture. The Swanscombe people, over a hundred thousand years ago, had skulls essentially like ours and a culture that barely changed for a thousand centuries. Skull dimensions do not reveal what a cortex can do — one anatomist compared reading capability from brain-case shape to phrenology.

> **30 · AI · 8s**
> ```
> Adult hands guiding a child's hands around a flint core by firelight, the strike
> happening together, other figures watching in the dark
> ```

What culture actually depends on is transmission. Shared tradition. The same techniques turn up across populations because they spread through contact, not because each group reinvented them. Human capacity is broadly shared; what differs between peoples is the tradition they inherited and the world it had to work in — not biology.
